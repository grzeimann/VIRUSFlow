# VIRUSFlow Scientific Knowledge Specification

# Working Note: Fiber Crosstalk and Long-Range Scattered Light

> Status: Initial scientific and architectural specification

This note defines fiber crosstalk as a physical redistribution of light between
detector locations and distinguishes two related components:

1. direct overlap between neighboring fiber cores, and
2. long-range scattered light produced by the extended wings of every fiber
   profile.

The second component is the dominant large-scale source of additive background
within a VIRUS amplifier.

---

# Scientific Interpretation

A fiber profile does not terminate at the edge of its bright core.

Imperfections in the fiber glass and optical system produce extended wings that
fall slowly with distance from the trace. Over sufficiently large distances,
these wings behave approximately like a power law rather than a compact
Gaussian-like profile.

For one fiber at one wavelength, the wing contribution may be small.

Across:

- all fibers,
- all wavelengths,
- and the full amplifier,

those contributions sum into a substantial diffuse background.

Conceptually:

```text
Observed Detector Image
    =
Direct Fiber Cores
    +
Neighbor-Core Crosstalk
    +
Long-Range Fiber Wings
    +
Other Background Terms
```

The accumulated long-range wing component is commonly described operationally
as scattered light.

---

# Two Crosstalk Regimes

## 1. Core-to-Core Crosstalk

Adjacent fibers are separated by approximately:

```text
8 to 8.5 detector pixels
```

The bright central fiber profile declines rapidly and is largely contained
within about four pixels of its trace center.

Because the half-separation is only slightly larger than the effective core
extent, neighboring profiles are not perfectly isolated.

This creates direct overlap between adjacent fiber cores.

Core crosstalk is:

- local,
- strongly dependent on fiber spacing,
- sensitive to profile width and asymmetry,
- and especially important for optimal extraction or aperture extraction.

## 2. Long-Range Scattered Crosstalk

Beyond the central profile, the extended wings decline slowly.

These wings contribute light well beyond immediately adjacent fibers.

The long-range component is:

- additive,
- spatially broad,
- generated by every illuminated fiber,
- dependent on the intensity distribution of the full amplifier,
- and capable of coupling distant fiber locations.

This component can dominate the inter-fiber background even when the
contribution from any single fiber is weak.

---

# Physical Modeling Unit: The CCD

The scattered-light phenomenon is a CCD-scale optical response, not an
amplifier-contained effect.

An amplifier boundary is a readout and storage boundary. It is not a physical
barrier to scattered light.

Light falling on one amplifier can therefore contribute extended wings to the
other amplifier on the same CCD.

For VIRUS, the relevant paired-amplifier modeling units are:

```text
Right CCD: RU + RL
Left CCD:  LU + LL
```

The true forward model must assemble each pair into the physical orientation of
the complete CCD before calculating distances or scatter contributions.

A model run independently on RU, RL, LU, or LL will necessarily omit light from
the paired amplifier and will be incomplete near—and potentially far from—the
amplifier boundary.

---

# Physical CCD Coordinates vs. Oriented Amplifier Coordinates

Pipeline amplifier images are commonly transformed into a convenient canonical
orientation in which:

- blue is on the left,
- red is on the right,
- and fiber order increases from bottom to top.

That orientation is appropriate for many amplifier-level algorithms.

It is not necessarily the physical orientation of the amplifier within the
CCD.

Crosstalk modeling must therefore distinguish:

```text
Canonical amplifier coordinates
```

from:

```text
Physical CCD coordinates
```

The scattered-light distance kernel must be evaluated in physical CCD
coordinates.

For each amplifier, the repository must know the transformation:

```text
T_amp:
    (x_amp, y_amp)
        →
    (x_ccd, y_ccd)
```

This transformation may include:

- axis reversal,
- rotation,
- translation,
- and placement relative to the paired amplifier.

Fiber order must not be used as a substitute for physical CCD orientation.

---

# CCD Assembly

Before scatter calculation, the algorithm should:

1. identify the two amplifiers belonging to the same CCD,
2. load their images, traces, spectra, and masks,
3. transform both amplifier datasets into physical CCD coordinates,
4. place all fiber traces into one shared coordinate system,
5. evaluate the scatter contribution from all fibers and wavelengths across the
   entire CCD,
6. and then transform the modeled result back into the native or canonical
   coordinate system of each amplifier.

Conceptually:

```text
RU + RL
    →
Right-CCD Physical Model
    →
RU Scatter Product + RL Scatter Product
```

and:

```text
LU + LL
    →
Left-CCD Physical Model
    →
LU Scatter Product + LL Scatter Product
```

The primary computation is CCD-level even if the final persisted images remain
amplifier-level Products.

---

# Cross-Amplifier Contributions

For a destination pixel on one amplifier, the scatter model must include:

- fibers on that amplifier,
- fibers on the paired amplifier,
- all sampled wavelengths from both amplifiers,
- and their true physical distances on the CCD.

Thus:

```text
Scatter_CCD(y, x)
    =
Σ over both amplifiers
Σ over all fibers
Σ over sampled wavelengths
Flux × Kernel(physical CCD distance)
```

The amplifier boundary must not truncate this sum.

This is especially important for:

- fibers near the amplifier boundary,
- very bright sources,
- long-range power-law wings,
- and measurements of the absolute scattered-light floor.

---

# Interpretation of the Existing Mirroring Approximation

The existing code duplicates the spectra and constructs reflected traces using
amplifier-dependent rules.

That operation should be understood as an approximation for missing
cross-boundary information.

It provides a synthetic continuation beyond the amplifier edge, but it does not
use the actual illumination pattern of the paired amplifier.

Consequently, it is most accurate when the paired amplifier has illumination
similar to the modeled amplifier, as may occur in relatively uniform laboratory
or flat-field exposures.

It can fail for science frames because the paired amplifier may contain:

- a different source distribution,
- different bright emission features,
- different masked regions,
- saturation,
- or substantially different total flux.

The proper replacement is not a more elaborate mirror.

It is explicit joint modeling of the two real amplifier datasets in physical
CCD coordinates.

---

# Physical Origin

The long-range profile is attributed primarily to imperfections and scattering
within the fiber and optical system.

Possible contributors include:

- inhomogeneity in the fiber glass,
- micro-defects,
- surface scattering,
- focal-ratio degradation,
- spectrograph optics,
- camera scattering,
- grating scatter,
- detector diffusion or charge redistribution,
- and wavelength-dependent optical behavior.

The operational model may be power-law-like without requiring that one physical
mechanism alone produces a perfect mathematical power law.

The measured wing is an empirical system response.

---

# Fiber Layout and Spatial Consequences

Detector adjacency is not equivalent to sky adjacency.

Fibers directly adjacent on the amplifier may belong to a common row in the IFU,
but that row structure repeats throughout the detector ordering.

As a result:

- nearby detector fibers can correspond to a patterned set of sky positions,
- repeated IFU rows can map into repeated fiber sequences,
- and a bright astronomical source illuminating a small spatial region may
  appear as a repeated detector-space pattern.

The scattered wings from those illuminated fibers then overlap many other
detector locations.

This can create a recognizable repeated or fence-like crosstalk pattern.

The mapping between:

```text
sky position
fiber identity
detector ordering
```

is therefore essential to interpreting crosstalk.

---

# Bright-Source Sensitivity

Crosstalk scales with source brightness.

For ordinary sky or continuum illumination, the additive contribution may appear
as a smooth amplifier background.

For a bright source:

- the direct neighboring fibers receive stronger contamination,
- the power-law wings become more visible,
- repeated fiber-layout patterns may emerge,
- and distant fibers can receive measurable additive flux.

A subtraction model that performs adequately for uniform calibration frames may
fail for science frames containing:

- bright stars,
- bright emission lines,
- compact galaxies,
- cosmic rays,
- saturated traces,
- or highly structured spectral energy distributions.

Science-frame behavior is therefore not simply a scaled version of flat-field
behavior.

---

# Spectrograph Dependence

The scattered-light response depends on the individual spectrograph.

VIRUS has 156 spectrographs, and the laboratory measurements indicate that
each spectrograph has its own best-fit coefficient values.

However, those solutions are similar in form and occupy only a narrow region of
parameter space.

The appropriate interpretation is therefore not:

```text
156 unrelated five-parameter models
```

but rather:

```text
one shared physical model family
    +
a small spectrograph-specific perturbation
```

The most economical implementation may use:

- one global default shape,
- one spectrograph-specific normalization,
- and, only if required by the data, one additional bounded shape perturbation.

This hierarchical representation preserves real spectrograph differences
without fitting poorly constrained independent models over an unnecessarily
large phase space.

---


# Laboratory-Constrained Power-Law Model

Masked laboratory flats were used to isolate selected fibers and directly
measure the extended detector response.

A successful empirical model was:

```python
def power_law(x, c1, c2=0.5, c3=0.15, c4=1.0, sig=2.5):
    return c1 / (c2 + c3 * abs(x / sig) ** c4)
```

For detector distance `r`, the response is:

```text
K(r) = c1 / [c2 + c3 |r / sig|^c4]
```

The parameters have distinct operational roles:

- `c1` controls the overall normalization,
- `c2` regularizes the central value,
- `c3` controls the relative strength of the extended component,
- `c4` sets the asymptotic power-law slope,
- `sig` sets the characteristic distance scale.

At large distance:

```text
K(r) ∝ r^(-c4)
```

The values used successfully in the detector-image calculation were:

```text
c1  = 1.4 × 10^-5
c2  = 0.5
c3  = 2.0
c4  = 1.0
sig = 1.5 pixels
```

With `c4 = 1`, the far wing approaches an inverse-distance response.

These coefficients should be understood as an empirical laboratory calibration,
not as a unique first-principles decomposition of the underlying optical
processes.

---

# Detector-Space Forward Calculation

The successful implementation evaluates the scatter in two detector dimensions,
not merely as cross-dispersion overlap at the same wavelength.

For a requested detector column and cross-dispersion position, it computes the
Euclidean distance to sampled locations along every fiber trace:

```text
distance =
sqrt[
    (y - trace_f(x'))^2
    +
    (x - x')^2
]
```

The modeled scattered light is then:

```text
Scatter(y, x)
    =
Σ_f Σ_x'
Spectrum_f(x') × K(distance)
```

Thus, every sampled wavelength from every fiber contributes to the predicted
background at the requested detector location.

This is important because the long-range response is a two-dimensional detector
halo, rather than only a one-dimensional leakage between fibers at a fixed
column.

The implementation samples the dispersion direction every 43 columns and
evaluates the cross-dispersion model at 25 detector positions. These sparse
samples make the otherwise expensive all-fiber, all-wavelength sum tractable.
The resulting model can then be interpolated onto a full detector grid.

---

# Boundary and Mirroring Approximation

The implementation augments the trace set with reflected traces.

For `LL` and `RU` amplifiers:

```text
trace_mirror = 2064 - trace
```

This is retained only as pre-refactor one-indexed characterization evidence.
The canonical zero-indexed image and trace transform is exactly
`trace_mirror = 2063 - trace`; `2064 - trace` is not an alternative convention.

For the other amplifier orientations:

```text
trace_mirror = -trace
```

The spectra are duplicated for the reflected traces.

This is a boundary approximation intended to include scatter contributions that
would otherwise lie outside the oriented 1032-pixel amplifier image.

A reimplementation must preserve the physical intent of this operation, but
should document and test whether the reflected spectrum is the correct
representation of the paired detector region or only an image-boundary
approximation.

The mirror convention depends on amplifier orientation and therefore must not be
replaced by one universal reflection rule.

---

# Low-Dimensional Spectrograph Model

Although the coefficients were measured separately for each spectrograph, the
solutions are sufficiently similar that the repository should begin with a
hierarchical model.

A suitable first representation is:

```text
K_s(r)
    =
A_s × K_global(r; θ_global)
```

where:

- `K_global` is the shared laboratory-derived shape,
- `A_s` is a spectrograph-specific amplitude,
- `θ_global` contains the fixed default shape parameters.

If one amplitude does not adequately describe the differences, add one small,
bounded perturbation:

```text
K_s(r)
    =
A_s × K_global(r; θ_global + δ_s)
```

where `δ_s` affects only the best-supported shape parameter, such as:

- the power-law slope `c4`,
- the radial scale `sig`,
- or the wing-strength term `c3`.

The repository should not permit all coefficients to vary freely by default.

That would make the model difficult to constrain and could fit noise or
science-frame structure rather than true spectrograph behavior.

---

# Relationship to Fiber Profiles

The fiber-profile note describes the bright central cross-dispersion profile.

Crosstalk extends that concept into the wings.

The complete detector response should conceptually include:

```text
Core Profile
    +
Intermediate Wings
    +
Long-Range Scattered Component
```

The current empirical core model is forced toward zero near approximately
±5 pixels.

That compact support is useful for extraction, but it deliberately excludes the
long-range power-law component.

Therefore:

> **The empirical core fiber profile and the scattered-light model are
> complementary Products, not competing descriptions.**

The core model explains how light is distributed locally.

The scatter model explains where the remaining long-range light goes.

---

# Additive vs. Multiplicative Calibration

Scattered light is additive.

Fiber normalization is multiplicative.

These must not be confused.

For detector counts:

```text
Observed Counts
    =
Fiber Throughput × True Fiber Signal
    +
Scattered Light
```

If scattered light is not removed before measuring fiber normalization, the
normalization can absorb part of the additive contribution.

That creates a physically incorrect Product.

The resulting fiber normalization may depend on:

- illumination level,
- neighboring-fiber brightness,
- spectral structure,
- and the scatter pattern.

This can misattribute light from multiple fibers to the fiber at the measured
detector location.

---

# Impact on Fiber Normalization

Suppose one detector location receives:

- direct light from its own fiber,
- local overlap from adjacent fibers,
- and diffuse wings from many other fibers.

If that total is treated as though all counts originated in the target fiber,
the inferred fiber response is biased.

This bias can vary with:

- illumination pattern,
- spectrograph,
- wavelength,
- source brightness,
- and fiber ordering.

Therefore, fiber normalization should ideally be measured after subtracting the
additive scattered-light contribution.

If not, the resulting Product must explicitly document that scattered light is
included.

---

# Impact on Science Data

Uncorrected crosstalk can:

- create false continuum,
- dilute absorption features,
- create weak false emission features,
- bias sky subtraction,
- alter extracted line ratios,
- contaminate faint fibers near bright sources,
- and create structured residuals tied to fiber ordering.

The effect is particularly dangerous when:

- a bright source illuminates only a few fibers,
- faint targets are extracted nearby in detector space,
- emission lines are much brighter than the local continuum,
- or sky subtraction assumes that additive light belongs to the receiving fiber.

---

# Impact on Sky Subtraction

Scattered light contains contributions from many fibers.

In a science exposure, those fibers may contain different combinations of:

- sky,
- object continuum,
- emission lines,
- calibration residuals.

The additive detector background is therefore not necessarily a pure sky
component.

If it is estimated and removed as a smooth background without accounting for
source structure, real object light may be subtracted from unrelated fibers.

Conversely, if it is left in place, sky and object spectra can inherit flux from
other detector locations.

---

# Why Science Frames Are Difficult

Calibration frames often provide:

- high signal,
- relatively smooth illumination,
- similar illumination across fibers,
- and predictable spectral structure.

Science frames can contain:

- large dynamic range,
- sparse bright sources,
- strong wavelength-localized emission,
- uneven sky illumination,
- saturated or masked pixels,
- and spatially structured targets.

Because scattered light is the sum of all illuminated profiles, the correct
model must respond to the actual fiber spectra in that exposure.

A static two-dimensional background image may not be sufficient.

The science-frame scattered light should ideally be predicted from:

```text
fiber intensities
    convolved with
spectrograph-specific extended profile model
```

---

# Forward-Model Interpretation

Crosstalk subtraction is naturally a forward-model problem.

For fiber `f` and wavelength or detector column `x`:

```text
Detector Contribution_f(y, x)
    =
Flux_f(x) × ExtendedProfile_f(y - trace_f(x), x)
```

The full scattered-light image is then:

```text
Scatter(y, x)
    =
sum over all fibers of
Flux_f(x) × WingProfile_f(y - trace_f(x), x)
```

This representation preserves the physical origin of the additive light.

It also makes clear that the model depends on:

- extracted or estimated fiber spectra,
- trace geometry,
- extended profile shape,
- wavelength,
- and spectrograph identity.

---

# Circularity Challenge

The scattered-light model depends on fiber flux.

But the extracted fiber flux depends on subtraction of scattered light.

This creates a circular problem:

```text
Need spectra to estimate scatter
Need scatter subtraction to estimate spectra
```

A robust implementation may therefore require iteration:

1. perform preliminary extraction,
2. predict scattered light,
3. subtract the model,
4. re-extract spectra,
5. update the scattered-light prediction,
6. stop when changes become negligible.

The preliminary extraction must be resistant to the very crosstalk being modeled.

---

# Model Decomposition

A practical model may separate:

## Local Core Crosstalk

Handled through:

- simultaneous extraction,
- empirical core profiles,
- neighboring-fiber deblending.

## Long-Range Scatter

Handled through:

- extended wing kernels,
- amplifier-wide forward modeling,
- or a low-dimensional scatter model tied to the fiber spectra.

These components overlap physically but may be treated separately
computationally.

The boundary must be explicit to avoid double subtraction.

---

# Required Physical Identity

The long-range scatter model is primarily spectrograph- and CCD-dependent.

Its physical identity should include at least:

```text
SPECID
CCD side or CCD identifier
```

where the CCD identifier distinguishes the paired units:

```text
RU + RL
LU + LL
```

The response may also depend on:

- wavelength,
- physical CCD position,
- fiber identity,
- temperature,
- focus,
- detector history,
- and instrument epoch.

AMP remains essential provenance and determines how CCD-level results are
partitioned for downstream use, but it is not the complete physical modeling
unit.

A conservative measured Product should preserve the complete ZipCodes of both
participating amplifiers.

The repository should determine empirically whether IFUID, IFUSLOT, controller,
or other hardware state must participate in the reusable model identity.

---

# Measurement Strategies

Potential ways to constrain the extended scatter include:

- high-signal continuum flats,
- twilight frames,
- isolated bright-fiber experiments,
- sparse illumination,
- bright emission-line exposures,
- stacked science residuals,
- and comparison between modeled cores and observed inter-fiber counts.

Each strategy constrains different aspects.

## Uniform Illumination

Useful for:

- total amplifier background,
- average wing strength,
- wavelength trends.

But difficult for uniquely assigning contributions because all fibers are
bright.

## Sparse Illumination

Useful for:

- direct measurement of the extended kernel,
- power-law slope,
- asymmetry,
- detector-distance dependence.

## Science Frames

Useful for validating:

- dynamic range,
- bright-source behavior,
- repeated fiber-layout patterns,
- subtraction residuals.

But they contain unknown astronomical structure.

---

# Required Algorithm Behavior

Any new scattered-light algorithm should:

- model the contribution as additive,
- use actual or iteratively estimated fiber intensities,
- operate jointly on both amplifiers of a physical CCD,
- transform amplifier data into verified physical CCD coordinates,
- include cross-amplifier scatter contributions,
- preserve spectrograph- and CCD-specific behavior,
- distinguish local core overlap from long-range wings,
- support strong dynamic range,
- avoid attributing source light to smooth background without evidence,
- handle masked and saturated pixels,
- preserve wavelength-dependent structure,
- and return modeled scattered-light images separately from corrected images.

The algorithm should not directly overwrite or persist data.

It should return a storage-neutral result.

---

# Architectural Execution Scope

The scattered-light calculation should be scheduled at CCD scope rather than
amplifier scope.

A suitable target identity is conceptually:

```text
ScatterTarget(
    observation_or_calibration_id,
    exposure_id,
    SPECID,
    CCD_ID
)
```

The target resolves exactly two participating amplifier lineages:

```text
Right CCD → RU and RL
Left CCD  → LU and LL
```

Both inputs must belong to the same exposure and instrument state.

The Task should fail rather than silently construct a complete model from only
one amplifier, unless it is explicitly operating in a documented degraded mode.

Required paired inputs include:

- both reduced amplifier images,
- both trace Products,
- both preliminary spectral Products,
- both pixel masks,
- physical CCD placement transforms,
- and the complete ZipCode provenance for each amplifier.

The algorithm returns one CCD-scale model. Persistence may then materialize
paired amplifier Products for compatibility with downstream amplifier-scoped
steps.

This creates an important distinction:

```text
Computation identity: SPECID + CCD_ID + exposure
Persistence identity: complete amplifier ZipCode + exposure
```

The model is computed jointly but can be consumed separately.

# Required QA

QA should evaluate:

- inter-fiber residual structure,
- residual correlation with bright fibers,
- residual repeated-pattern or fence structure,
- residual dependence on detector distance from bright traces,
- fraction of counts attributed to scatter,
- subtraction stability across iterations,
- negative residual regions,
- preservation of object flux,
- impact on fiber normalization,
- and improvement in extraction residuals.

A visually smooth residual is not sufficient.

Over-subtraction can also produce a smooth image.

---

# Critical Validation Tests

A trustworthy model should pass tests such as:

## Flux Conservation

The subtraction should redistribute attribution without destroying total light
beyond modeled losses or masked boundaries.

## Bright-Source Injection

Synthetic bright fibers should produce the expected local and long-range
crosstalk pattern.

## Blank-Fiber Test

Fibers without direct illumination should not retain scatter correlated with
bright detector neighbors after correction.

## Fiber-Normalization Stability

Fiber normalization should become less dependent on illumination level after
scatter subtraction.

## Science Preservation

Real source flux should not be removed merely because it contributes to a broad
detector pattern.

## Spectrograph Specificity

A model trained on one spectrograph should not be assumed valid for another
without validation.

# Initial Implementation Decisions

Until better evidence is available:

- Treat crosstalk as an additive detector-space contribution.
- Separate direct neighboring-core overlap from long-range scattered wings.
- Preserve the empirical compact core profile for extraction.
- Model long-range scatter separately.
- Assume scatter strength depends on the actual fiber spectra.
- Use the physical CCD, not an individual amplifier, as the forward-modeling
  unit.
- Jointly model RU with RL and LU with LL.
- Transform both amplifiers into physical CCD coordinates before computing
  distances.
- Use actual paired-amplifier illumination rather than mirrored duplicate
  spectra.
- Use a shared laboratory-derived model family with a small
  spectrograph- and CCD-specific perturbation.
- Begin with spectrograph-specific normalization and fixed global shape.
- Add at most one bounded shape perturbation unless evidence supports greater
  complexity.
- Preserve complete hardware and environmental provenance.
- Subtract scatter before deriving multiplicative fiber normalization whenever
  possible.
- Never silently absorb additive scatter into fiber throughput.
- Return the scatter model and diagnostics explicitly.
- Validate science-frame behavior separately from calibration-frame behavior.
- Use iterative extraction and subtraction when required.
- Define the boundary between core and scatter models explicitly to prevent
  double counting.
